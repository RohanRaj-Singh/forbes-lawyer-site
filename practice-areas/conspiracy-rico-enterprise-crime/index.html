Conspiracy, RICO & Enterprise Crime

Conspiracy 6th Degree (PL § 105.00)

Conspiracy in the sixth degree (Penal Law § 105.00) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of agreeing with at least one other person to commit any crime, even a misdemeanor, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward carrying it out (Penal Law § 105.20). Prosecutors may allege that group texts, social media messages, or what police saw before an arrest show you planned a shoplifting trip, a graffiti run, a protest action, or a fight with friends, and it can carry up to three months in jail and a criminal record, usually alongside the charge for the underlying crime. Conspiracy 5th Degree (PL § 105.05)

Conspiracy in the fifth degree (Penal Law § 105.05) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of agreeing with others to commit a felony, most often a lower-level felony such as grand larceny, third-degree burglary, forgery, or identity theft, or of an adult agreeing with someone under 16 to commit any crime, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward the plan (Penal Law § 105.20). Prosecutors may allege phone records, group chats, surveillance video, or a co-defendant’s statements show you were part of a plan to steal, break into a building, use stolen card numbers, or pass fake documents, even if the crime was never completed, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, a criminal record, and a separate conviction on top of any charge for the crime itself. Conspiracy 4th Degree (PL § 105.10)

Conspiracy in the fourth degree (Penal Law § 105.10) is a New York felony that accuses someone of agreeing with others to commit a class B or C felony, such as a robbery, a home burglary, a large theft, a shooting, or a gun or drug sale, or of an adult agreeing with someone under 16 to commit any felony, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward the plan. Prosecutors may allege wiretapped calls, text messages, surveillance, or a cooperating witness tie you to a plan carried out or attempted by others, even if your role was driving, scouting a location, or holding a phone, and it can carry up to four years in state prison and a felony record, usually alongside charges for the underlying crime. Conspiracy 3rd Degree (PL § 105.13)

Conspiracy in the third degree (Penal Law § 105.13) is a New York felony that accuses an adult 18 or older of agreeing with someone under 16 to commit a class B or C felony, such as a robbery, a burglary, a shooting, or a gun or drug sale, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward the plan. Prosecutors use it against adults they claim recruited young teenagers to carry guns, hold drugs, or take part in robberies, because minors face lighter consequences. Prosecutors may allege that group chats, social media, or statements from the young people involved show you planned the crime with them or directed them, even if you did not know their ages or were barely older yourself, and it can carry up to seven years in state prison, a felony record, and a gang-recruitment label that follows you through the case. Conspiracy 2nd Degree (PL § 105.15)

Conspiracy in the second degree (Penal Law § 105.15) is a New York felony that accuses someone of agreeing with others to commit a class A felony, such as murder, kidnapping, first-degree arson, or the sale or possession of large amounts of drugs, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward the plan. It is the conspiracy charge prosecutors bring in gang retaliation plots, murder-for-hire stings, and major drug takedowns. Prosecutors may allege that wiretaps, recorded jail calls, text messages, social media posts, or a cooperating witness prove you agreed to a killing, a kidnapping, or a large drug deal, even if no one was ever harmed and nothing was sold, and it can carry up to 25 years in state prison and a felony record, usually alongside other serious charges. Conspiracy 1st Degree (PL § 105.17)

Conspiracy in the first degree (Penal Law § 105.17) is New York’s most serious conspiracy charge, a class A-I felony that accuses a person over 18 of agreeing with someone under 16 to commit a class A felony, such as murder, kidnapping, or a major drug crime, with someone in the group taking at least one step toward the plan. Prosecutors bring it in gang cases where they claim older members directed young teenagers to carry out shootings or other serious violence. Prosecutors may allege that messages, social media, jail calls, or statements from the young people involved prove you planned the crime with them or gave the orders, even if you were barely an adult or the teenagers acted on their own, and it carries a sentence of up to life in state prison, with 15 to 25 years served before parole eligibility. Charged With Conspiracy for a Crime That Never Happened (PL §§ 105.20, 110.00)

Being charged with conspiracy for a crime that never happened is possible in New York because conspiracy is complete once two or more people agree to commit a crime and any one of them takes a single overt act toward it (Penal Law § 105.20), even if the planned robbery, shooting, theft, or drug deal never takes place. When police step in partway through, prosecutors often stack conspiracy with attempt charges (Penal Law § 110.00), and in sting cases the other “conspirator” may have been an informant or undercover officer from the start. Prosecutors may allege that a text, a phone call, a meeting, a purchase, or a drive past a location was enough to complete the conspiracy, even if you changed your mind or never meant to follow through, and it can carry anything from up to three months in jail for conspiring to commit a misdemeanor to up to 25 years, or life, in state prison for conspiring to commit the most serious felonies. Charged as an Accessory or for Aiding and Abetting: Accomplice Liability (PL § 20.00)

Being charged as an accessory or for aiding and abetting in New York means prosecutors are using the state’s accomplice liability law (Penal Law § 20.00), called acting in concert, which makes you guilty of the same crime as the person who committed it if you shared their intent and intentionally helped, asked, or encouraged them. New York has no separate “accessory” crime, so a lookout, a getaway driver, or someone who held a weapon or stolen property faces the same charge and the same sentence as the person who carried out the act. Prosecutors may allege that being in the car, standing nearby, passing a phone, sharing a group chat, or leaving with the others after a robbery, assault, or theft proves you were part of it, even though the law says presence alone is not enough, and it can carry the full sentence for the underlying crime, from a misdemeanor to decades in state prison. Criminal Facilitation 4th Degree (PL § 115.00)

Criminal facilitation in the fourth degree (Penal Law § 115.00) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of helping another person commit a felony by providing the means or opportunity, while believing it was probable that person intended to commit a crime, or of an adult helping someone under 16 commit any crime. Unlike accomplice liability, prosecutors do not have to prove you shared the other person’s goal, only that you thought a crime was likely and that your help actually made it possible, which is why facilitation is often offered as a reduced plea in felony cases. Prosecutors may allege you lent your car, phone, apartment, or bank account, gave someone a ride, or pointed a buyer to a seller while suspecting what they planned to do, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail and a misdemeanor record. Prosecutors use it when they claim an adult gave a teenager a gun, a car, a ride, drugs to sell, or a place to hide stolen property, without having to prove the adult shared the teenager’s goal. Prosecutors may allege you lent a car, held or handed over a weapon, or drove a younger relative or neighbor somewhere knowing trouble was likely, even if you did not know the teenager’s exact age or plan, and it can carry up to four years in state prison and a felony record. Criminal Facilitation 2nd Degree (PL § 115.05)

Charged With Asking Someone to Commit a Crime: Criminal Solicitation (PL §§ 100.00-100.13)

Criminal solicitation (Penal Law §§ 100.00-100.13) accuses someone of asking, urging, paying, or ordering another person to commit a crime, and unlike conspiracy, the other person does not have to agree and the crime does not have to happen. The charge ranges from a violation for asking someone to commit a minor crime to a felony for asking someone to commit a serious felony or for an adult soliciting someone under 16, with the most serious degrees reserved for soliciting a murder, kidnapping, or other class A felony. Prosecutors may allege that a text, a social media message, a recorded call, or a conversation with an undercover officer or informant shows you tried to get someone to steal, burn property, hurt someone, or sell drugs, and it can carry up to 15 years in state prison at the highest degree, a criminal record, and conspiracy charges if the other person went along with it. Harboring a Fugitive or Helping Someone Avoid Arrest: Hindering Prosecution (PL §§ 205.55, 205.60)

Harboring a fugitive or helping someone avoid arrest is charged in New York as hindering prosecution (Penal Law §§ 205.55, 205.60), which accuses someone of hiding a person they know committed a felony, warning them that police are coming, giving them money, a ride, a phone, or a disguise, or getting rid of evidence. It is a misdemeanor when the underlying crime is any felony and a felony when the person helped is wanted for a serious felony such as a robbery, a burglary, a first-degree assault, or a shooting, and New York law makes no exception for family members. Prosecutors may allege you let a son, partner, friend, or relative stay with you after an arrest warrant was issued, told them detectives were looking for them, or drove them out of the city, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail for hindering in the third degree and up to four years in state prison for hindering in the second degree, along with a record that ties you to someone else’s case. State RICO Charges: Enterprise Corruption (PL § 460.20)

Enterprise corruption (Penal Law § 460.20) is New York’s state RICO law, a felony that accuses someone of knowingly taking part in a criminal enterprise, meaning a structured group with an ongoing criminal purpose, through a pattern of at least three criminal acts, including at least two felonies, within ten years (Penal Law § 460.10). District attorneys and the Attorney General use it against gangs and crews, illegal gambling and loansharking operations, organized theft and fraud rings, and businesses they claim were run as criminal organizations. Prosecutors may allege that your phone calls, social media, financial records, or presence at a business or on a block tie you to an organization and its crimes, even if you were charged with only a few of the underlying acts, and it can carry up to 25 years in state prison, forfeiture of money and property tied to the enterprise (Penal Law § 460.30), and the use of evidence about everyone else’s crimes against you at trial. Mafia, Mob, and Organized Crime Charges (PL § 460.20)

Mafia, mob, and organized crime charges in New York are usually brought as enterprise corruption, the state’s RICO law (Penal Law § 460.20), along with promoting gambling, criminal usury, extortion, coercion, and money laundering, by district attorneys and the Attorney General who claim the defendants are members or associates of one of the Five Families or another organized crime group. These cases often grow out of years of wiretaps, surveillance, and informants, and they frequently run alongside, or ahead of, federal racketeering investigations. Bookies, Illegal Gambling, and Poker Game Raids (PL Article 225)

Bookmaking, illegal gambling, and poker game charges in New York are brought as promoting gambling (Penal Law §§ 225.05, 225.10) and possession of gambling records (Penal Law §§ 225.15, 225.20). These charges target the people who run or profit from the game, such as bookies, collectors, game hosts, and dealers who take a rake or seat fee, rather than the players. It becomes a felony when a bookie takes more than five bets totaling over $5,000 in a single day, and larger operations are charged as enterprise corruption with money laundering. Loan Shark Charges: Criminal Usury (PL §§ 190.40, 190.42)

Loan sharking is charged in New York as criminal usury (Penal Law §§ 190.40, 190.42), which makes it a felony to knowingly charge or collect interest above 25 percent a year on a loan, and a more serious felony when the lender has a prior usury conviction or makes and collects these loans as a business. Lenders accused of collecting through threats can also face extortion and coercion charges, and larger operations are charged as enterprise corruption. Extortion Charges (PL §§ 155.30, 155.40)

Extortion is charged in New York as grand larceny by extortion (Penal Law §§ 155.30(6), 155.40(2)), which accuses someone of getting money or property by making a person afraid of what will happen if they do not pay, such as violence, property damage, being reported to police or immigration, or having a secret or embarrassing images exposed. Any extortion is a felony regardless of the amount, and it becomes a more serious felony when the threat involves physical injury, property damage, or a public official’s power. Prosecutors may allege that a demand to repay a debt, a threat in a business or romantic dispute, a message about releasing photos, or a protection payment from a store owner was extortion, often relying on texts, recorded calls, and payment records, and it can carry up to four years in state prison for grand larceny in the fourth degree, up to 15 years for grand larceny in the second degree, and enterprise corruption charges when prosecutors claim a group was behind it. Burglary Rings and Robbery Crews (PL Article 105; PL § 460.20)

Burglary rings and robbery crews are prosecuted in New York by alleging the underlying crimes against those for whom the prosecution can establish direct or accessorial liability, with conspiracy charged where its distinct elements are met (Penal Law Article 105), and, when prosecutors claim an organized group, enterprise corruption (Penal Law § 460.20), with stolen property charges for anyone who resold the goods. Recent cases have targeted crews accused of breaking into high-end homes across New York City, Long Island, and Westchester, smash-and-grab jewelry store robberies, and moped crews snatching phones on city streets. It is often stacked on the fraud charges themselves and, in larger rings, on enterprise corruption, while federal prosecutors bring similar cases as wire fraud conspiracy. Conspiracy and terroristic threat charges accuse two or more people of planning together, often in a group chat, Discord server, or social media thread, to threaten or attack a school, synagogue, mosque, church, or other public place. Teen Charged in a Gang Case (CPL Article 722)

When a teenager is charged in a gang case in New York, 16- and 17-year-olds accused of felony conspiracy, gang assault, shootings, or robberies are prosecuted as adolescent offenders in the Youth Part of Supreme Court under Raise the Age (Criminal Procedure Law Article 722), and children 13 to 15 can be charged as juvenile offenders for murder and certain violent felonies (Penal Law § 30.00). Caught on a Wiretap: Eavesdropping Warrant Cases (CPL Article 700)

Being caught on a wiretap in New York means police listened to your calls or read your texts under an eavesdropping warrant (Criminal Procedure Law Article 700), the backbone of most conspiracy, gang, and enterprise corruption cases. The law sets strict rules: a judge must find that ordinary investigative methods would not work, officers must minimize interceptions of calls that have nothing to do with the crime, and the recordings must be sealed as soon as the warrant ends, and a violation of these rules can get the recordings thrown out. Prosecutors may allege that slang, nicknames, and short phrases on intercepted calls are coded talk about guns, drugs, or violence, often relying on a detective’s interpretation of what you meant, and it can tie you to every crime in the conspiracy, supporting charges that carry up to 25 years in state prison or more. Named in a Multi-Defendant Indictment (CPL § 200.40)

Being named in a multi-defendant indictment in New York means prosecutors have charged you in a single case with a group of other people, often dozens, usually on conspiracy or enterprise corruption counts that tie everyone to the same alleged plan (Criminal Procedure Law § 200.40). In these cases the evidence against the most active defendants can spill over onto everyone else unless your lawyer moves to sever your case and have it tried separately. Prosecutors may allege that appearing in the same photos, group chats, phone records, or neighborhood as the main targets makes you part of the conspiracy, and it can mean defending against thousands of pages of discovery and hours of recordings, “package” plea offers that require every defendant to plead guilty, co-defendants cooperating against you, and sentences of up to 25 years in state prison on the top counts. Forbes Stranger defends individuals named in multi-defendant indictments in New York State Supreme Court in every borough. When your name is buried in a list of dozens of defendants, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Co-Defendant Is Snitching or Cooperating Against You (CPL § 60.22)

When a co-defendant starts snitching or cooperating against you, someone charged in your case has signed a deal with prosecutors to testify about what you did in exchange for a lighter sentence, usually after proffer sessions where they tell investigators everything they know. New York law does not allow a conviction based only on an accomplice’s word, so prosecutors must find independent evidence connecting you to the crime (Criminal Procedure Law § 60.22), and they must turn over every promise and benefit given to the cooperator (Criminal Procedure Law § 245.20). Prosecutors may allege that the cooperator’s account of meetings, phone calls, and who gave the orders proves your role in the conspiracy, even though cooperators have every reason to exaggerate what others did and minimize their own part, and it can mean a stronger case at trial, rising pressure to plead, and sentences of up to 25 years in state prison on conspiracy and enterprise counts. Forbes Stranger defends individuals in every borough facing cooperating co-defendants. When someone trades your freedom for their own, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Bank Accounts Frozen or Property Seized in a RICO Case (PL § 460.30; CPLR Article 13-A)

Bank accounts get frozen and property gets seized in New York RICO cases because prosecutors can ask a judge, often without notice to you, to restrain money and property they claim is tied to an enterprise corruption charge, and then seek to forfeit it permanently after a conviction (Penal Law § 460.30) or in a separate civil case (CPLR Article 13-A). The freeze can hit personal and business accounts, homes, cars, cash, jewelry, and cryptocurrency, including property held in the names of spouses, parents, or business partners. Prosecutors may allege that your savings, your business, or your family’s home was bought with or used for the enterprise’s crimes, and it can leave you unable to pay rent, employees, or a lawyer while the case moves forward, and cost you everything you own after a conviction, including property you earned legitimately. Forbes Stranger defends individuals, business owners, and family members in every borough whose accounts and property have been frozen in enterprise corruption cases. When prosecutors freeze everything you own before you have had your day in court, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Business Owners Charged in a RICO or Enterprise Case (PL §§ 20.20, 460.20)

Business owners charged in a RICO or enterprise case in New York face enterprise corruption charges (Penal Law § 460.20) built on the claim that their company was run as, or used by, a criminal enterprise, and both the owners and the business itself can be prosecuted (Penal Law §§ 20.20, 20.25). These cases have targeted construction and trucking companies, car dealerships, medical clinics, lenders, smoke shops, and real estate businesses, often after years of undercover work, subpoenas, and cooperating employees. Prosecutors may allege that payments to workers or vendors, cash deposits, loan terms, billing practices, or a single employee’s misconduct prove the business was part of a pattern of crimes, and it can carry up to 25 years in state prison for the owners, forfeiture of the company and its assets, loss of licenses and public contracts, and a court-appointed monitor overseeing the business. Forbes Stranger defends business owners, executives, managers, and companies in enterprise corruption investigations and prosecutions in every borough. Pulled Out Before the Crime Happened: The Renunciation Defense (PL § 40.10)

The renunciation defense (Penal Law § 40.10) allows someone charged with conspiracy, solicitation, facilitation, attempt, or accomplice liability to avoid conviction by proving they voluntarily and completely abandoned the plan and, depending on the charge, stopped the crime from happening or made a substantial effort to prevent it. It is an affirmative defense, so the defense has to prove it, and it does not apply if you backed out only because you feared getting caught or wanted to wait for a better time (Penal Law § 40.10(5)). Prosecutors may allege that you stayed in the group chat, kept your share of the money, or stopped answering calls only once police got close, arguing you never truly withdrew, and without a strong renunciation defense it can mean conviction for the full conspiracy and responsibility for everything the others did after you walked away, with sentences of up to 25 years in state prison on the top counts. Forbes Stranger defends individuals in every borough who walked away from a plan before it happened. When you walked away and prosecutors still charge you, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Just Being There Is Not a Crime: The Mere Presence Defense (PL § 20.00)

The mere presence defense rests on a long-settled rule of New York law: being at the scene of a crime, knowing it was happening, or associating with the people who committed it is not enough to make you guilty as an accomplice (Penal Law § 20.00) or as a conspirator (Penal Law Article 105). Prosecutors must prove you shared the intent and actually helped or agreed, and juries are instructed that presence alone cannot support a conviction. Prosecutors may allege that being in the car, standing on the corner, living in the apartment, appearing in the group chat, or showing up in photos with the people charged proves you were part of the plan, and without a defense built around that line it can mean conviction for the same crimes as everyone else, with sentences of up to 25 years in state prison on conspiracy and enterprise counts. Forbes Stranger defends friends, family members, neighbors, and individuals in every borough who were charged for being in the wrong place with the wrong people. When being there is the only thing prosecutors can prove, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.
