How to Appeal a Misdemeanor Conviction in NYC (CPL Article 450)

Appealing a misdemeanor conviction in New York City means asking the Appellate Term, the court that reviews Criminal Court cases from Manhattan and the Bronx (First Department) and from Brooklyn, Queens, and Staten Island (Second Department), to reverse the conviction or reduce the sentence (Criminal Procedure Law Article 450). The notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of sentencing (Criminal Procedure Law § 460.10), and the appeal is decided on the written record and the briefs, not through a new trial. Prosecutors may allege that any mistakes at trial or at the plea were harmless or were never properly objected to, and if the conviction stands it can leave you with a criminal record, immigration consequences, lost job and housing opportunities, and a sentence you are still serving, from probation to jail. How to Appeal a Felony Conviction in New York (CPL Article 450)

Appealing a felony conviction in New York means asking the Appellate Division to reverse the conviction, order a new trial, or reduce the sentence (Criminal Procedure Law Article 450). The First Department hears cases from Manhattan and the Bronx, and the Second Department hears cases from Brooklyn, Queens, Staten Island, Westchester, and Long Island. It is the only New York court that can review the facts, overturn a verdict as against the weight of the evidence, and cut a sentence it finds too harsh, but the notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of sentencing (Criminal Procedure Law § 460.10). Prosecutors may allege that the evidence was overwhelming, that any errors were harmless, or that your trial lawyer failed to preserve the issues, and if the conviction stands it can mean years or decades in state prison, post-release supervision, and a felony record that follows you for life. Appealing to New York’s Highest Court (CPL § 460.20)

Appealing to New York’s highest court means asking the New York Court of Appeals in Albany to review your case after the Appellate Division has ruled, which in almost every criminal case first requires permission, called leave to appeal, from a single judge (Criminal Procedure Law § 460.20). The application must be made within 30 days after the Appellate Division’s decision is served, and the court considers only questions of law, such as constitutional violations and legal errors that matter beyond your own case. Prosecutors may allege that your case raises no question worth the court’s time or that the Appellate Division got it right, and because the court grants leave in only a small share of criminal cases, a weak or late application can end your state appeals and jeopardize your chance at federal habeas review. Missed the 30-Day Deadline to Appeal (CPL § 460.30)

Missing the 30-day deadline to file a notice of appeal does not always end your right to appeal in New York, because a court can extend the deadline if you move within one year after it passed and show the appeal was not filed because of your lawyer’s failure, a court or jail official’s mistake, or your inability to reach your lawyer (Criminal Procedure Law § 460.30). If your lawyer promised to file and never did, and you could not have learned about it in time, New York’s highest court has allowed a late appeal even after the one-year window (People v. Syville). Prosecutors may allege that you knew about the deadline, chose not to appeal, or waited too long, and if the court denies the extension it can lock in the conviction and the sentence for good, with no chance to raise the errors that could have overturned it. Getting Out of Jail While Your Appeal Is Pending (CPL § 460.50)

Getting out of jail while your appeal is pending means asking an appellate judge to stay your sentence and release you on bail or on your own recognizance until the appeal is decided (Criminal Procedure Law § 460.50), which the judge can grant when the appeal raises real issues and you are not a flight risk (Criminal Procedure Law § 530.50). Police Searched You Illegally and the Judge Let the Evidence In (CPL § 710.70(2))

When police searched you illegally and the judge refused to suppress the evidence, that ruling can be challenged on appeal, and in New York the right to appeal it survives even a guilty plea (Criminal Procedure Law § 710.70(2)). Convicted Without Enough Evidence: Legal Sufficiency and Weight of the Evidence (CPL § 470.15(4)(b), (5))

Being convicted without enough evidence can be challenged on appeal in New York in two ways: by arguing that the proof was legally insufficient to establish every element of the crime, or by asking the Appellate Division to reweigh the evidence and the witnesses’ credibility and find that the verdict went against the weight of the evidence (Criminal Procedure Law § 470.15(5); People v. Unfair Jury or Juror Misconduct (CPL § 330.30)

An unfair jury or juror misconduct can be grounds to overturn a conviction in New York when the judge kept a juror who could not promise to be fair (Criminal Procedure Law § 270.20), the prosecution struck jurors because of their race or gender (Batson v. Misconduct discovered after the verdict can be raised in a motion to set aside the verdict before sentencing (Criminal Procedure Law § 330.30), and later on appeal or in a motion to vacate. When the prosecutor hid evidence or crossed the line, a conviction can be overturned in New York if prosecutors withheld evidence that could have helped the defense, such as a witness’s deal, a prior inconsistent statement, or proof pointing to someone else (Brady v. Your Case Took Too Long: Speedy Trial Violations (CPL § 30.30)

A preserved statutory speedy-trial claim may require dismissal before trial; after conviction, relief depends on preservation and the distinct rules governing constitutional speedy-trial and ineffective-assistance claims. Prosecutors must actually be ready for trial within six months on a felony, 90 days on a class A misdemeanor, 60 days on a class B misdemeanor, and 30 days on a violation (CPL § 30.30), and readiness is ordinarily dependent on a valid discovery certificate, whose sufficiency is now assessed under CPL § 245.50’s due-diligence and good-faith framework, with specified challenge procedures and exceptions. Even when the statutory clock was met, years of delay can still violate your constitutional right to a speedy trial (CPL § 30.20; People v. Taranovich). That constitutional claim survives a guilty plea and a waiver of appeal, and a lawyer who never filed a winning speedy trial motion can be grounds to vacate the conviction. Sentenced Too Harshly: Getting Your Sentence Reduced on Appeal (CPL § 470.15(6)(b))

A New York appeals court can reduce a sentence that is legal but too harsh. It can do that in the interest of justice even when the judge made no legal mistake (CPL § 470.15(6)(b)), and it does not have to defer to the sentencing judge (People v. Delgado). Appeals courts shorten prison terms, change consecutive sentences to concurrent ones, and correct sentences that punished someone for going to trial instead of pleading guilty. The state’s highest court has no power to do any of this, so your first appeal is the only chance. Can I Appeal After Pleading Guilty?

Yes, you can appeal after pleading guilty in New York. A plea gives up many claims, but not the right to argue that the plea itself was not knowing and voluntary, including when the judge never explained the rights you were giving up (People v. Tyrell), never told you about postrelease supervision (People v. Catu), or never warned you that the plea could get you deported (People v. Peque). A guilty plea also leaves several other appeals open: the denial of a motion to suppress evidence (CPL § 710.70(2)), an illegal sentence, a constitutional speedy trial violation, a sentencing promise the court did not keep, and a misdemeanor charge that never properly alleged a crime (People v. Dreyden). Prosecutors may allege that your plea waived every issue, that you told the judge under oath you were guilty and satisfied with your lawyer, or that you never moved to withdraw the plea, and if the plea stands it can mean a permanent conviction, prison time, and deportation for a deal you never truly understood. Signed a Waiver of Appeal? You May Still Be Able to Appeal (People v. Thomas)

Signing a waiver of appeal does not always end your right to appeal. Waivers are invalid when the judge rushed through them, relied on a form you signed without explaining it, or told you that you were giving up every right to appeal when some rights can never be waived (People v. Thomas; People v. Bradshaw), and once a waiver falls, every issue it was meant to block, including a request to reduce your sentence, is back on the table. Even a valid waiver does not bar challenges to an illegal sentence, an involuntary plea, a constitutional speedy trial violation, or your competence to plead guilty (People v. Callahan). Prosecutors may allege that you signed the waiver, told the judge you understood it, and gave up every argument you now want to make, and if the waiver is enforced it can mean serving every day of a sentence no court ever reviewed. How to Get a Conviction Overturned: Motion to Vacate (CPL § 440.10)

A motion to vacate is the main way to overturn a New York conviction once the appeal is over or when the problem is something the trial record never shows, and it can be filed at any time, even decades later (CPL § 440.10). My Lawyer Messed Up My Case: Ineffective Assistance of Counsel

If your lawyer messed up your case, a conviction can be overturned for ineffective assistance of counsel when the lawyer’s mistakes denied you a fair trial or a fair plea (Strickland v. Washington). New evidence or DNA that could clear you can overturn a New York conviction when the evidence could not have been found before trial and would probably have changed the verdict (CPL § 440.10(1)(g)), and judges must order DNA testing of the evidence whenever there is a reasonable probability that the results would have led to a better outcome, including in some cases after a guilty plea (CPL § 440.30(1-a)). Illegal Sentence: Getting a Sentence Fixed (CPL § 440.20)

Parole Board Hearing: How to Get Released on Parole (Executive Law § 259-i)

At a parole board hearing, the Board decides whether you are likely to stay out of trouble, whether your release is compatible with public safety, and whether release would make light of the seriousness of the crime, and it must weigh your institutional record, programs, release plans, risk assessment, and sentencing minutes, not just the crime itself (Executive Law § 259-i(2)(c)(A)). Probation Violations and Hearings (CPL § 410.70)

A probation violation can be appealed, and both the finding and the new sentence reversed, when the prosecution never proved the violation by a preponderance of the evidence, when the judge relied on hearsay alone or on a new arrest that was never proven, or when you never got written notice of the charges and a fair hearing (CPL § 410.70; Gagnon v. A certificate of relief from disabilities removes the automatic bars a conviction places on jobs and professional licenses for anyone with no more than one felony, and a certificate of good conduct does the same for people with two or more felonies after a waiting period (Correction Law §§ 701-703-b).
