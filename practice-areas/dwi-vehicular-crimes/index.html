DWI & Vehicular Crimes

DWAI: Driving While Ability Impaired by Alcohol (VTL § 1192(1))

Driving while ability impaired by alcohol (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(1)), known as DWAI, is a New York traffic infraction that accuses someone of driving after drinking enough to impair their ability to drive to any extent, usually shown by a blood alcohol content between .05 and .07. It is not a crime on a first or second offense, but it becomes a misdemeanor if it is your third alcohol-related driving offense within ten years (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1193(1)(a)). Prosecutors may allege an officer smelled alcohol, saw you drift between lanes or roll through a stop sign, or recorded a breath test in the DWAI range, and it can carry a fine of up to $500, up to 15 days in jail, a 90-day license suspension, a $750 DMV driver responsibility assessment, higher insurance rates, and a conviction that counts against you if you are ever charged again. Forbes Stranger defends drivers, professionals, commercial drivers, and individuals charged with DWAI in every borough’s criminal court. DWI and DUI Charges: Driving While Intoxicated (VTL § 1192(2), (3))

Driving while intoxicated (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(2), (3)), known as DWI and searched by many as DUI, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving with a blood alcohol content of .08 or higher, or of driving while intoxicated based on how they looked, spoke, and performed on roadside tests, even without a breath result. It is the most common drunk driving charge in New York and the starting point for every more serious DWI offense. Prosecutors may allege a breath or blood test, slurred speech, the smell of alcohol, or poor field sobriety tests after a stop, a checkpoint, or a crash prove you were intoxicated, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, fines of up to $1,000 plus surcharges, at least a six-month license revocation, a required ignition interlock device, a $750 DMV driver responsibility assessment, and a criminal record. Forbes Stranger defends drivers, professionals, commercial drivers, students, and individuals charged with DWI in every borough’s criminal court. When one night of drinking puts your license, your job, and your record on the line, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Aggravated DWI: .18 BAC or Higher (VTL § 1192(2-a)(a))

Aggravated driving while intoxicated (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(2-a)(a)) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving with a blood alcohol content of .18 or higher, more than twice the legal limit, and the law restricts plea bargains so that an aggravated DWI generally cannot be reduced below a regular DWI (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(10)). Prosecutors may allege a breath or blood test taken after a stop, a checkpoint, or a crash showed .18 or higher, even though machine calibration and the timing of the test can push results up, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, fines of up to $2,500, at least a one-year license revocation, a required ignition interlock device, and felony charges if you have a prior DWI within ten years. Forbes Stranger defends drivers, professionals, commercial drivers, and individuals charged with aggravated DWI in every borough’s criminal court. When one number on a breath test raises every penalty, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Driving High or Drugged Driving: DWAI-Drugs (VTL § 1192(4))

Driving while ability impaired by drugs (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(4)), known as DWAI-Drugs, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving while their ability was impaired by marijuana, prescription medication such as Xanax, Ambien, or oxycodone, or an illegal drug like cocaine or heroin. Unlike alcohol, marijuana and other drugs have no legal limit, so these cases often rest on an officer’s observations and a drug recognition expert’s opinion rather than a reliable test. Prosecutors may allege that the smell of cannabis, red eyes, a drug recognition evaluation, a blood or urine test, or pills found in the car prove you were impaired, even though THC and many medications stay in the body long after their effects wear off, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, fines of up to $1,000, at least a six-month license suspension, and a misdemeanor criminal record. Forbes Stranger defends medical cannabis patients, people taking prescribed medication, rideshare and delivery drivers, and individuals charged with DWAI-Drugs in every borough’s criminal court. Drunk and High: DWAI Combined Alcohol and Drugs (VTL § 1192(4-a))

Driving while ability impaired by the combined influence of drugs, or of alcohol and drugs (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192(4-a)), is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving while impaired by a mix of substances, such as a few drinks and marijuana, or alcohol and a prescription medication. Prosecutors often use it when a breath test comes back under .08 but they believe drugs made up the difference. Prosecutors may allege that a low breath test, the smell of alcohol or cannabis, a drug recognition evaluation, or a blood test showing medication or THC proves the combination impaired you, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, fines of up to $1,000, at least a six-month license revocation, and a misdemeanor record that can raise the stakes in any future DWI case. The offense can be prosecuted as a felony and may carry imprisonment, license consequences, and related child-protection concerns. Second DWI: Felony DWI Within 10 Years (VTL § 1193(1)(c)(i))

A second DWI within ten years is charged in New York as a felony (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1193(1)(c)(i)): any DWI, aggravated DWI, or drug-impaired driving charge becomes a class E felony when you have a prior conviction for one of those offenses in the last ten years, including a conviction from another state. A prior DWAI conviction does not make a new DWI a felony, which is why how the first case ended matters so much. Prosecutors may allege your new arrest and your old conviction together make you a repeat offender, and it can carry up to four years in state prison, fines of up to $5,000, at least a one-year license revocation, a required ignition interlock device, and, if the prior was within five years, a mandatory five days in jail or 30 days of community service. Third DWI: Class D Felony DWI (VTL § 1193(1)(c)(ii))

A third DWI within ten years is charged in New York as a class D felony (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1193(1)(c)(ii)) when you have two or more prior DWI, aggravated DWI, or drug-impaired driving convictions in the last ten years, including convictions from other states. It is the most serious DWI charge New York brings when no one was hurt, and it puts your right to drive at risk for the rest of your life under DMV’s permanent revocation rules. Prosecutors may allege your record shows a pattern of impaired driving that only prison will stop, and it can carry up to seven years in state prison, fines of up to $10,000, a ten-day jail or 60-day service requirement only where the separate five-year lookback and qualifying-conviction conditions are satisfied, a required ignition interlock device, and a license revocation that DMV may refuse to ever lift. Under 21 DWI and Zero Tolerance (VTL § 1192-a)

Under New York’s Zero Tolerance law (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192-a), a driver under 21 with a blood alcohol content of just .02 to .07 faces a DMV hearing and license suspension even though it is not a crime, while a reading of .05 or higher can bring DWAI charges and .08 or higher brings a criminal DWI. Any alcohol-related driving conviction for someone under 21 also carries at least a one-year license revocation. Prosecutors may allege you drove after drinking at a party, at a bar you entered with a fake ID, or at a college event, and it can carry a six-month suspension and a civil penalty for a Zero Tolerance finding, a one-year revocation for refusing a breath test, up to 364 days in jail for a DWI conviction, and consequences for school, scholarships, and future jobs. CDL DWI: Commercial Drivers Charged With DWI (VTL §§ 1192, 510-a)

Commercial drivers charged with DWI in New York face the same DWI laws as everyone else (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192), plus a lower .04 blood alcohol limit while driving a commercial vehicle and separate CDL disqualification rules (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 510-a). A DWI conviction or a chemical test refusal, even in your personal car, disqualifies your CDL for at least one year, and a second can mean lifetime disqualification. Prosecutors may allege you were impaired while driving a truck, bus, or tractor-trailer, or while off duty in your own car, and it can carry the full criminal penalties of a DWI, the loss of your CDL and your job, and no conditional license that lets you keep driving commercially while your case is pending. Boating While Intoxicated (BWI) and Boating DUI (Navigation Law § 49-a)

Boating while intoxicated (Navigation Law § 49-a), known as BWI or boating DUI, is a New York crime that accuses someone of operating a boat, jet ski, or other vessel with a blood alcohol content of .08 or higher or while impaired by drugs, with lower-level boating while ability impaired charges at .05 and felony charges for a repeat offense within ten years. The U.S. Coast Guard can bring separate federal boating under the influence charges, and the vehicular assault and manslaughter laws that apply to cars also apply to boats when someone is hurt. Prosecutors may allege the NYPD Harbor Unit, a state or county marine patrol, or the Coast Guard stopped your boat or jet ski and found you intoxicated after a day on the water, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, fines of up to $1,000, the suspension or revocation of your boating privileges, a criminal record, and felony charges for a repeat offense or a crash with injuries. Refused a Breathalyzer: Chemical Test Refusal and DMV Refusal Hearings (VTL § 1194(2))

Refusing a breathalyzer or other chemical test after a DWI arrest in New York triggers a separate DMV case under the implied consent law (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1194(2)), which begins with a license suspension at arraignment and a DMV refusal hearing governed by VTL § 1194(2)(c); if DMV fails to provide a hearing within 15 days after arraignment, driving privileges must be reinstated pending the hearing. Refusing the small roadside breath device is only a traffic infraction, but refusing the full test at the precinct is what can cost you your license for a year. Prosecutors may allege you refused after being clearly warned of the consequences, and it can carry a license revocation of at least one year, or 18 months with a prior, a $500 civil penalty, a $750 driver responsibility assessment, and the use of your refusal at trial as evidence that you knew you were impaired, with the DMV penalties applying even if the criminal case is dismissed. Arrested at a DWI Checkpoint (VTL § 1192)

A DWI checkpoint arrest happens when police stop cars at a roadblock without suspecting anyone of a crime, which New York allows only if the checkpoint follows a neutral written plan approved by supervisors and stops cars in a set pattern (People v. Scott). Drivers who show signs of drinking are pulled aside for field sobriety and breath tests that lead to DWI charges (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192). Prosecutors may allege that the smell of alcohol, red eyes, an open container, or an admission to having “a couple of drinks” during a brief checkpoint conversation justified the tests that followed, and it can carry the full penalties of a DWI, including up to 364 days in jail, license revocation, a required ignition interlock device, and added charges for a suspended license or anything found in the car. License Suspended After a DWI Arrest: Suspension Pending Prosecution and Hardship Licenses (VTL § 1193(2)(e)(7))

A license suspension after a DWI arrest usually happens at your first court date, when the judge must suspend your license until the case ends if a chemical test showed .08 or higher or if you have a recent prior DWI (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1193(2)(e)(7)), and a refusal brings its own suspension until the DMV hearing. The only way to keep driving right away is a hardship license, which requires proving at a hearing within days that losing your license would cause extreme hardship and that you have no other way to get to work, school, or medical care. Prosecutors may allege you cannot show extreme hardship because family members can drive you or public transit is available, a hard argument to beat in New York City, and it can mean weeks or months without a license before your case is resolved, the loss of a job that depends on driving, and new criminal charges if you drive while suspended. Getting a Conditional License After a DWI: Impaired Driver Program (VTL § 1196)

A conditional license (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1196) lets someone whose license was suspended or revoked after a DWI or DWAI drive to and from work, school, medical appointments, child care, and court-ordered programs, as long as they enroll in New York’s Impaired Driver Program, a state alcohol and drug education course with screening and possible treatment. It is often available after a conviction, or 30 days into a suspension pending prosecution, but not to drivers who took part in the program within the last five years, and it never covers commercial driving. The DMV may allege you are ineligible because of a prior program, a prior conviction, or the type of offense, and it can mean months with no license at all, the immediate loss of a conditional license for driving outside its limits, and new aggravated unlicensed operation charges if you are caught behind the wheel. Ignition Interlock After a DWI (VTL § 1198)

An ignition interlock device is a breath-testing unit wired into your car’s ignition, and New York requires everyone convicted of DWI, aggravated DWI, or a Leandra’s Law felony to install one in any vehicle they own or drive (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1198; Penal Law § 60.21), usually for at least 12 months, with possible removal after six months of clean results. The car will not start if the device detects alcohol, it demands random retests while you drive, and every result is reported to probation or a monitor. Prosecutors may allege a failed or missed test, a skipped calibration appointment, or driving a car without the device violates your sentence, and it can bring hundreds of dollars a month in device costs, a longer interlock period, a violation of probation or conditional discharge, jail time, and new misdemeanor charges. Caught Driving Without Your Ignition Interlock (VTL § 1198(9))

Driving without a required ignition interlock is a New York misdemeanor (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1198(9)) that accuses someone under an interlock order of driving a car without the device, tampering with or disconnecting it, or having someone else blow into it to start the car. The same law also covers anyone who lends a car without a device to a restricted driver or blows into the device for them, and these cases usually begin with a traffic stop, a monitor report, or a probation check. Prosecutors may allege you drove a friend’s, partner’s, or work vehicle without an interlock, skipped installation, or had someone else provide the breath sample, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail, a fine of up to $1,000, resentencing on the original DWI through a probation or conditional discharge violation, and new DWI charges if alcohol is involved. Lost Your License for Multiple DWIs: DMV Permanent Revocation (15 NYCRR Part 136)

Losing your license for multiple DWIs in New York usually comes from the DMV’s lifetime review rules (15 NYCRR Part 136), which look back 25 years when a driver with a revoked license applies to get it back. Five or more alcohol- or drug-related driving convictions or refusals in a lifetime, or three or four within 25 years plus a serious driving offense, can mean permanent denial, while three or four without a serious offense can mean an extra five-year wait followed by five years on a restricted license with an ignition interlock. The DMV may allege that your record, including old out-of-state convictions and refusals, puts you in a permanent or extended revocation category, and it can mean never driving legally in New York again, the loss of any job that requires a license, and new criminal charges, from aggravated unlicensed operation to felony DWI, if you drive anyway. Police Took Your Car After a DWI Arrest (NYC Admin. Code § 14-140)

When police take your car after a DWI arrest in New York City, the NYPD can hold it as the instrumentality of a crime and the city can sue to forfeit it permanently (New York City Administrative Code § 14-140), whether you own the car or borrowed it from a family member or employer. Owners have the right to a prompt Krimstock hearing to challenge the city’s hold on the car while the case moves forward, and some suburban counties run their own DWI seizure programs. The city may allege the car should be kept because of your blood alcohol level, a refusal, or a prior DWI, and it can mean losing the car for months or for good, paying towing and storage fees, owing payments on a car you cannot use, and statements in the forfeiture case being used against you in the criminal case. Driving Without a License (VTL § 509)

Driving without a license (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 509) is a New York traffic offense that accuses someone of driving without a valid license for the vehicle they were operating, whether they never had one, let one expire, drove on a learner permit without a licensed driver, or rode a motorcycle without the right endorsement. If DMV records show your license was suspended or revoked, police usually charge the more serious crime of aggravated unlicensed operation instead. Prosecutors may allege you knew you were not licensed, or that your license from another state or country was no longer valid because you live in New York, and it can carry fines and possible jail time that increase with each offense, the towing of your car, higher insurance costs, and, if a suspension turns up, misdemeanor or felony charges. Driving With a Suspended License: AUO 3rd Degree (VTL § 511(1))

Aggravated unlicensed operation in the third degree (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 511(1)), known as AUO 3rd, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving while knowing, or having reason to know, that their license was suspended or revoked, and a suspension notice mailed by the DMV is usually enough for prosecutors to claim you knew. It is one of the most common charges after a traffic stop in New York City, and the suspension often traces back to something small, like an unpaid ticket, a missed court date, a lapse in insurance, or unpaid child support. Prosecutors may allege DMV records and notices prove you knew your license was suspended, even if you moved and never received the letter, and it can carry up to 30 days in jail, fines of up to $500, the towing or impound of your car, and a misdemeanor criminal record that can follow you into job and immigration applications. Aggravated Unlicensed Operation 2nd Degree (AUO 2nd, VTL § 511(2))

Aggravated unlicensed operation in the second degree (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 511(2)), known as AUO 2nd, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving on a suspended or revoked license in one of three situations: the suspension came from a DWI arrest or conviction or a chemical test refusal, they have three or more suspensions on separate dates for unpaid tickets or missed court dates, or they have an AUO conviction in the past 18 months. Prosecutors may allege that driving to work, picking up a child, or moving your car while your license was suspended after a DWI or refusal shows disregard for the court’s order, and it can carry a mandatory fine of $500 to $1,000, up to 180 days in jail or probation, a longer revocation, and felony charges if you are also impaired. Aggravated Unlicensed Operation 1st Degree (AUO 1st, VTL § 511(3))

Aggravated unlicensed operation in the first degree (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 511(3)), known as AUO 1st, is a New York felony that accuses someone of driving on a suspended or revoked license while also drunk, high, or impaired, or of driving with ten or more suspensions on separate dates for unpaid tickets or missed court dates. Fake or Fraudulent Driver’s License Charges (PL § 170.25)

A fake or fraudulent driver’s license charge in New York usually means criminal possession of a forged instrument in the second degree (Penal Law § 170.25), a felony, because a driver’s license is an official government document, and lying on a DMV application or holding licenses under two names can also bring charges of offering a false instrument for filing (Penal Law § 175.35). Underage students who use a fake ID to get into a bar can face the same felony charge, not just an alcohol violation. Speeding Tickets and License Points That Can Suspend Your License (VTL § 510)

Speeding tickets and other moving violations in New York carry DMV points, and 11 points within 18 months, three speeding tickets within 18 months, or a single ticket for driving more than 40 miles per hour over the limit can lead to the suspension or revocation of your license (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 510). Vehicular assault in the second degree (Penal Law § 120.03) is a New York felony that accuses someone of causing serious physical injury to another person, such as a serious head injury, internal injuries, or lasting impairment, while driving drunk or impaired by drugs, and the law presumes the impairment caused the crash unless the evidence shows otherwise. It applies to injured passengers, other drivers, pedestrians, and cyclists, and it also covers boats and snowmobiles. Vehicular assault in the first degree (Penal Law § 120.04) is a New York felony that accuses someone of causing serious physical injury while driving drunk or impaired with an aggravating factor, such as a blood alcohol content of .18 or higher, a suspended or revoked license, a prior DWI within ten years, injuries to more than one person, or a child 15 or younger in the car. It is charged after crashes that prosecutors treat as far more than an accident, and it is often filed alongside aggravated DWI, AUO, or Leandra’s Law counts. Prosecutors must prove reckless driving on top of intoxication, such as extreme speeding, running red lights, or weaving through traffic. Arrested for DWI After a Car Accident (VTL §§ 1192, 1194)

An arrest for DWI after a car accident usually starts when police respond to a crash, smell alcohol or hear an admission, and ask for a breath test, which New York allows from any driver involved in an accident (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1194(1)(b)), with a court-ordered blood test available if someone is seriously hurt and you refuse (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1194(3)). Even a minor fender-bender can lead to DWI charges, and a crash with injuries can bring felony vehicular assault. Hit a Pedestrian or Cyclist: NYC Right of Way Law (NYC Admin. Code § 19-190)

Hitting a pedestrian or cyclist in New York City can lead to a criminal charge under the city’s Right of Way Law (New York City Administrative Code § 19-190), which makes it a misdemeanor to fail to yield to someone in a crosswalk or bike lane who had the right of way and cause them physical injury, even without alcohol, speeding, or any intent to harm. Hit-and-Run: Leaving the Scene of an Accident (VTL § 600)

Hit-and-run in New York is charged as leaving the scene of an incident (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 600), which requires any driver who knows or has reason to know their car damaged property or injured someone to stop, show their license and insurance, and report to police. Leaving after property damage is a traffic infraction, leaving after someone is hurt is a misdemeanor, and leaving after a serious injury is a felony. Prosecutors may allege traffic cameras, license plate readers, surveillance video, or paint transfer and damage on your car connect you to a crash you may not have realized happened, or that you drove off in a panic, and it can carry up to 15 days in jail for property damage, up to three months for an injury case and more for a repeat offense, mandatory license revocation when someone was hurt, and added DWI charges if police claim you left to sober up. Felony Hit-and-Run With Serious Injury (VTL § 600(2))

Felony hit-and-run is charged in New York as leaving the scene of an incident resulting in serious physical injury (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 600(2)), a class E felony that accuses a driver of leaving, without stopping, identifying themselves, and reporting to police, after a crash that seriously injured a pedestrian, cyclist, passenger, or other driver, when they knew or had reason to know someone was hurt. These cases are investigated by the NYPD’s Collision Investigation Squad, and they are often charged alongside vehicular assault when police believe the driver was impaired. Reckless Driving (VTL § 1212)

Reckless driving (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1212) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of driving in a way that unreasonably endangers other drivers, pedestrians, or cyclists, or unreasonably interferes with the use of the road, such as extreme speeding, weaving through traffic, tailgating, or racing. It is a criminal charge, not just a ticket, and it is often added to DWI, fleeing, or street racing cases or offered as a reduced plea. Prosecutors may allege that an officer’s observations, dashcam or body camera footage, or a crash prove your driving crossed from careless to reckless, and it can carry up to 30 days in jail for a first offense and up to 180 days for repeat offenses, fines, five points on your license, higher insurance costs, and a misdemeanor record. Running From the Police in a Car: Unlawful Fleeing 3rd Degree (PL § 270.25)

Unlawful fleeing a police officer in a motor vehicle in the third degree (Penal Law § 270.25) is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of trying to get away from a uniformed officer or a marked police car that signaled them to stop with lights or a siren, by driving at least 25 miles per hour over the speed limit or driving recklessly. Unlawful Fleeing a Police Officer 2nd Degree (PL § 270.30)

Unlawful fleeing a police officer in a motor vehicle in the second degree (Penal Law § 270.30) is a New York felony that accuses someone of fleeing police in a car at high speed or recklessly and causing serious physical injury to another person during the chase, whether a pedestrian, another driver, a passenger, or an officer. Fake paper plates, ghost cars, and toll evasion charges target drivers whose cars cannot be traced by cameras. First Offense DWI in New York (VTL § 1192)

A first offense DWI in New York (Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1192) is a misdemeanor, and while first-time drivers rarely go to jail, the case still moves fast: a license suspension at arraignment, a DMV refusal hearing if you declined the test, and pressure to plead guilty before the evidence has been tested. Arrested on a Moped, Scooter, or E-Bike in NYC (VTL §§ 401, 509, 511, 1192)

DWI charges for non-citizens and green card holders can carry immigration consequences that many lawyers miss, even though a single, simple DWI usually does not lead to deportation on its own. How to Fight a DWI: Challenging the Breathalyzer and Field Sobriety Tests (VTL § 1194)

Fighting a DWI in New York means testing every piece of the prosecution’s case, starting with the stop itself and moving to the breath test, including whether the test was lawfully obtained under Vehicle and Traffic Law § 1194, whether a qualified operator used a properly calibrated machine, and whether the required observation procedures were followed. Field sobriety tests like the walk-and-turn, the one-leg stand, and the eye-tracking test are reliable only when officers give them exactly by the book, and in New York City many are recorded on video at the NYPD’s Intoxicated Driver Testing Unit.
