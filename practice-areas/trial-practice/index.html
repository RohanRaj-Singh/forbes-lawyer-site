Hiring a Private Lawyer for Trial

Hiring a private lawyer for trial means retaining your own defense attorney to take over from a public defender, Legal Aid, or an 18-B assigned lawyer. The Constitution protects your right to choose your own counsel, so judges generally must allow the switch as long as it is not made just to delay the trial (United States v. Can I Change Lawyers Right Before Trial? Switching Counsel for Trial (People v. Arroyo)

You can change lawyers right before trial in New York, even shortly before jury selection. Asking the judge to assign a different free lawyer requires showing good cause (People v. Sides), but hiring a private lawyer of your own is a constitutional right the court must respect unless the change is a delay tactic (People v. Arroyo). People switch lawyers because their lawyer has not reviewed the discovery, has not investigated or interviewed witnesses, will not return calls or visit Rikers, or keeps pushing a plea when they want a trial, and the earlier you switch, the more time your new lawyer has to prepare and the easier it is to get any adjournment the trial requires. Will My Trial Be Postponed If I Hire a New Lawyer? (People v. Arroyo)

Hiring a new lawyer may postpone your trial. Judges often grant a reasonable adjournment so new counsel can review the discovery and prepare, but the decision is up to the court, which weighs how long the case has been pending, whether witnesses are ready, and whether the change looks like a delay tactic (People v. Arroyo). Time the defense asks for is usually excluded from the prosecution’s speedy trial clock (CPL § 30.30(4)(b)), so a postponement can mean more time in custody for someone held on bail, but in many cases a new lawyer who is hired early and gets the file quickly can be ready on the existing trial date. Prosecutors may allege that you are shopping for lawyers to stall, that victims and witnesses have waited long enough, or that the trial must go forward as scheduled, and if the request is handled poorly you can be forced to trial with a lawyer who had no time to prepare. Jury Trial or Bench Trial in New York (CPL § 320.10)

In New York, felony cases are tried before a jury of 12 and class A misdemeanor cases before a jury of six, unless you give up that right in a signed writing the judge approves (CPL §§ 320.10, 360.10). Class B misdemeanors charged by information now carry a statutory jury-trial right in New York City under CPL § 340.40(2), subject to a valid waiver and statutory exceptions, and every jury verdict must be unanimous. A bench trial can be the better choice when the defense turns on a legal issue, the facts are disturbing enough to inflame a jury, or the judge has a record of holding the prosecution to its burden, while a jury is often better when the case comes down to whether police officers or other witnesses are telling the truth. Suppression Hearing Lawyer: Mapp, Huntley, and Wade Hearings (CPL § 710.60)

A suppression hearing is a mini-trial held before your trial, where the judge decides whether evidence can be used against you. That includes physical evidence from a search (Mapp), your statements to police (Huntley), identifications from lineups, photo arrays, and showups (Wade), and anything that flowed from an arrest without probable cause (Dunaway) (CPL § 710.60(4)). The prosecution must call the officers to justify what they did, which gives the defense its first chance to cross-examine them under oath and lock in their testimony for trial, and when the judge suppresses the key evidence, the case is often dismissed. Prosecutors may allege that the officers had reasonable suspicion or probable cause, that you waived your rights, or that the identification was fair, and if the evidence comes in it can carry the case straight to a conviction and years in prison. Retrial After a Hung Jury or Mistrial (CPL § 310.60)

After a hung jury or mistrial, prosecutors can usually retry the case, and the speedy trial clock starts again from the date of the mistrial (CPL §§ 30.30(5)(a), 310.60). A mistrial declared over the defense’s objection without true necessity, or one provoked by prosecutorial misconduct, can bar a retrial under double jeopardy (CPL § 40.30; Oregon v. Kennedy). A retrial also gives the defense something it never had before, the full transcript of every prosecution witness, and with it the chance to expose every inconsistency, fix what did not work, and build on the jurors who already voted to acquit. Prosecutors may allege that the first jury came close to convicting, that they have strengthened their case, or that you should plead before a second trial, and a conviction at retrial can carry the same full sentence the first trial threatened. Federal Criminal Trial Lawyer in NYC (18 U.S.C. § 3161)

A federal criminal trial in New York City takes place in the Southern District of New York in Manhattan or the Eastern District of New York in Brooklyn, before a jury of 12 that must reach a unanimous verdict. The rules differ sharply from state court, including the Speedy Trial Act’s 70-day clock and its many exclusions (18 U.S.C. § 3161) and the Jencks Act, which lets prosecutors hold back witness statements until shortly before those witnesses testify (18 U.S.C. § 3500). Federal prosecutors build their cases over months or years with wiretaps, cooperating witnesses, and financial records, and a conviction at trial usually costs you the reduction for accepting responsibility under the Sentencing Guidelines (U.S.S.G. § 3E1.1), so the defense has to be ready from the day the trial date is set. Federal prosecutors may allege that recordings, cooperators, and documents prove your guilt beyond question, and a federal conviction can carry mandatory minimum sentences, years in federal prison with no parole, forfeiture, and deportation for non-citizens. Fighting a Confession at Trial (CPL § 60.45)
