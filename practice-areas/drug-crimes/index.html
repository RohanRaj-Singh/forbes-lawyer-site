Criminal Possession of a Controlled Substance 7th Degree (CPCS 7th, PL § 220.03)

Criminal possession of a controlled substance in the seventh degree (Penal Law § 220.03), known as CPCS 7th, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of knowingly possessing any amount of an illegal drug, including cocaine, crack, heroin, fentanyl, ketamine, molly, or pills without a prescription, and it is the most common drug charge in New York City. Prosecutors may allege you knowingly had a small bag, a single pill, or even residue in a pipe in your pocket, your car, or your bag, often found during a stop, a search, or a night out, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail and a drug conviction that can lead to deportation for non-citizens and problems with jobs, housing, and professional licenses. Forbes Stranger defends students, professionals, workers, non-citizens, and individuals charged with CPCS 7th in every borough’s criminal court. When a single pill or a small bag puts your record on the line, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Criminal Possession of a Controlled Substance 5th Degree (CPCS 5th, PL § 220.06)

Criminal possession of a controlled substance in the fifth degree (Penal Law § 220.06), known as CPCS 5th, is a New York felony that accuses someone of possessing any illegal drug with intent to sell it, or of possessing amounts the law treats as felony weight, including half a gram or more of cocaine or more than a gram of ketamine. That weight is measured by the entire mixture, not the pure drug. Prosecutors may allege a few small bags of cocaine or ketamine put you over the felony weight, or that cash, a scale, or empty baggies found with the drugs show you were selling, and it can carry up to two and a half years in state prison for a first offense, longer with a prior felony, and a felony drug record. Forbes Stranger defends students, young adults, workers, and individuals charged with CPCS 5th in every borough. When the weight on a lab report turns a misdemeanor into a felony, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Drug Money Laundering Charges (Penal Law §§ 470.05-470.20)

Fentanyl Charges (PL Article 220)

Fentanyl charges in New York are brought under the same drug laws as heroin and cocaine (Penal Law Article 220), because fentanyl is a narcotic drug, and the weight that sets the charge includes everything it is mixed with, including heroin, cutting agents, and pressed pills. Possessing any amount is a crime, possessing an eighth of an ounce or more is a felony, selling any amount is criminal sale in the third degree, and the charges become class A felonies once the weight reaches ounces. Prosecutors may allege glassines, bundles, or blue “M30” pills that tested positive for fentanyl were yours, or that a sale or a package traces back to you, and it can carry up to nine years in state prison for a first sale or intent-to-sell charge, up to 20 years at the highest weights, manslaughter charges if someone dies, and federal mandatory minimums of five or ten years when federal prosecutors take the case (21 U.S.C. § 841). Forbes Stranger defends young adults, workers, people struggling with addiction, and individuals charged with fentanyl possession and sale in state and federal court in every borough. A fake prescription charge in New York usually means prosecutors claim you forged, altered, or used a false prescription to get controlled substances, and because the law treats a prescription like an official document, it is charged as criminal possession of a forged instrument in the second degree (Penal Law § 170.25), a felony. Prosecutors may allege you changed the quantity on a script, used a stolen prescription pad or a doctor’s e-prescribing credentials, called in a fake prescription, or filled prescriptions at several pharmacies, and it can carry up to seven years in state prison on a forged instrument charge, separate drug possession charges for the pills themselves, and a record that ends careers in healthcare and other licensed fields. Drug Paraphernalia: CUDP 2nd Degree (PL § 220.50)

Criminally using drug paraphernalia in the second degree (Penal Law § 220.50), known as CUDP 2nd, is a New York misdemeanor that accuses someone of possessing or selling scales, empty baggies, glassines, vials, or capsules, or cutting agents such as mannitol or lactose, under circumstances suggesting they were meant for packaging or mixing drugs for sale. Pipes and syringes are not covered by this law, though residue inside a pipe can be charged as drug possession. Prosecutors may allege that a digital scale in your kitchen, a box of small baggies in your car, or packaging found near cash or drugs shows you were dealing, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail and is often used to support more serious intent-to-sell charges. Forbes Stranger defends young adults, workers, shop owners, and individuals charged with CUDP 2nd in every borough’s criminal court. When a kitchen scale or a box of baggies becomes evidence of dealing, Forbes Stranger is the firm you want standing between you and the prosecution. Call Forbes Stranger now.

Drug Paraphernalia: CUDP 1st Degree (PL § 220.55)

Criminally using drug paraphernalia in the first degree (Penal Law § 220.55), known as CUDP 1st, is a New York felony that accuses someone of possessing or selling scales, baggies, vials, or cutting agents meant for packaging or mixing drugs after a prior conviction for CUDP 2nd, so what would otherwise be a misdemeanor becomes a felony because of your record. Prosecutors may allege the same kind of scale or packaging that led to an earlier misdemeanor plea was found on you again, and it can carry up to two and a half years in state prison and a felony record, often alongside possession and intent-to-sell charges. Charged With Bringing Drugs Into Rikers or a Prison: Promoting Prison Contraband (PL §§ 205.20, 205.25)

Bringing drugs into Rikers Island, a state prison, or a local jail is charged in New York as promoting prison contraband (Penal Law §§ 205.20, 205.25), a misdemeanor for ordinary contraband and a class D felony for “dangerous contraband,” a category prosecutors routinely apply to fentanyl, heroin, K2, and Suboxone strips. The same charges apply to people in custody found with drugs and to officers and staff accused of smuggling them in. Prosecutors may allege you passed drugs during a visit, hid them in clothing, food, or your body, mailed letters or legal papers soaked in K2, or received drugs in your cell, and it can carry up to 364 days in jail for second-degree contraband, up to seven years in state prison for first-degree contraband, the loss of visiting privileges, and, for officers and staff, bribery charges and the end of a career. Smoke Shop Raided, Padlocked, or Charged (Cannabis Law §§ 132, 138-b)

A smoke shop raid in New York means city or state agencies claim your store sold cannabis without a license, and under the Cannabis Law (Cannabis Law §§ 132, 138-b) they can padlock the store after an inspection, seize all inventory, and seek civil penalties of up to $10,000 a day, rising to $20,000 a day after an order to stop, plus up to five times the revenue from the sales. Sales can also bring criminal charges under New York’s cannabis laws (Penal Law Article 222), especially for large quantities or sales to anyone under 21, along with tax and tobacco license violations. Prosecutors and regulators may allege your store sold weed, vapes, or edibles over the counter or through a “gifting” setup, or that you sold to teenagers, and it can carry crippling daily fines, the loss of your inventory and your lease, revocation of your tobacco licenses, misdemeanor or felony charges, and landlord penalties that push your landlord to evict you. Arrested for Selling Drugs to an Undercover Cop (Buy-and-Bust) (PL § 220.39)

A buy-and-bust arrest happens when an undercover NYPD officer buys drugs on the street, radios a description of the seller to a backup team, and has that team arrest whoever matches it minutes later, usually on a charge of criminal sale of a controlled substance in the third degree (Penal Law § 220.39) for heroin, cocaine, crack, or fentanyl. The case often rests on a quick look by the undercover officer, because the pre-recorded buy money and the drugs are frequently never found on the person arrested. Prosecutors may allege you made the sale, acted as a steerer who pointed the officer to a seller, or held the drugs or the money for someone else, and it can carry up to nine years in state prison for a first offense, longer with a prior felony, and a felony drug sale record, even when the only evidence is the officer’s identification. Charged With Selling Drugs on Snapchat, Telegram, or a Delivery Service (PL §§ 220.31-220.43)

Selling drugs through Snapchat, Telegram, Instagram, WhatsApp, or a delivery service is charged in New York as criminal sale of a controlled substance (Penal Law §§ 220.31-220.43), and because New York defines selling to include offering or agreeing to sell, a message offering drugs can be charged as a sale even if nothing was ever delivered. Delivery services are often charged as conspiracies (Penal Law Article 105) that reach dispatchers, drivers, and the people who run the phones. Prosecutors may allege your account posted a drug menu, that an undercover officer ordered from you through an app, or that you drove orders across the city for a delivery service, often relying on search warrants for your messages, your Cash App, Venmo, or Zelle records, and your phone’s location data, and it can carry up to nine years in state prison for a first sale of cocaine, fentanyl, or pills containing them, more time for larger amounts or with a prior felony, and federal charges when the operation crosses state lines or uses the mail. Police Found Drugs in Your Car (PL § 220.25(1))

Drugs found in a vehicle can create a statutory presumption of possession against its occupants (Penal Law § 220.25(1)). Police Raided Your Home and Found Drugs (PL § 220.25(2))

When police raid a home with a search warrant and find drugs, New York law presumes that everyone close to drugs left in open view knowingly possessed them, if the scene suggests the drugs were being mixed, packaged, or prepared for sale (Penal Law § 220.25(2)). That can lead to possession, intent-to-sell, and paraphernalia charges against every adult in the apartment, from tenants and roommates to guests who happened to be there. Prosecutors may allege drugs, scales, baggies, or cash found in a kitchen, bedroom, or common area belonged to you, often based on a warrant obtained through a confidential informant or a controlled buy, and it can carry up to nine years in state prison for intent to sell, three to 20 years when the weight reaches class A felony levels, NYCHA or landlord eviction proceedings, and ACS investigations when children live in the home. Arrested in a Fentanyl Mill Raid (PL §§ 220.21, 220.25(2))

Being arrested in a fentanyl mill raid means police and federal agents say an apartment or house was being used to cut, bag, and stamp fentanyl or heroin for street sale, and everyone found inside is usually charged under New York’s room presumption (Penal Law § 220.25(2)) with criminal possession of a controlled substance in the first or third degree, drug paraphernalia, and conspiracy. Many of these cases are brought by the Special Narcotics Prosecutor with the DEA, and some go to federal court with added charges for maintaining a drug premises (21 U.S.C. § 856). Arrested for a Package of Drugs Mailed to You (PL Article 220)

Being arrested for a package of drugs mailed to you usually follows a controlled delivery: postal inspectors, customs officers, or shipping carriers find drugs in a package, and police posing as delivery workers hand it over and arrest whoever signs for it, opens it, or brings it inside. The charge is criminal possession of a controlled substance (Penal Law Article 220) based on the weight in the box, or attempted possession when police swapped out the drugs, and federal prosecutors can add distribution and mail charges (21 U.S.C. §§ 841, 843(b)). Arrested for Drugs at a Club, Concert, or Festival (PL §§ 220.03, 220.31, 220.39)

Being arrested for drugs at a club, concert, or festival in New York usually begins when venue security finds pills, powder, or capsules during a bag check or pat-down and turns you over to police, or when undercover officers working the crowd see a handoff. It leads to drug possession charges (Penal Law § 220.03) or, if anything was shared, drug sale charges (Penal Law §§ 220.31, 220.39), because New York treats splitting drugs with friends as a sale. Arrested After Calling 911 for an Overdose (PL § 220.78)

New York’s Good Samaritan law (Penal Law § 220.78) provides a qualified bar to specified prosecutions based on evidence obtained as a result of a good-faith request for overdose-related medical help for someone who is overdosing, or for yourself, and it covers every level of drug possession except a class A-I felony, along with paraphernalia and underage drinking. Sale charges are not covered, but someone who called for help can raise the 911 call as an affirmative defense to most sale charges if they have no prior serious drug felony. Prosecutors may allege the law does not protect you because they claim you sold or supplied the drugs, had enough drugs for a class A-I charge, or were not acting in good faith, and officers who respond to an overdose often arrest everyone in the room anyway, which can lead to possession and sale charges carrying up to nine years in state prison and, if someone dies, a manslaughter investigation. Licensed professionals charged with drug crimes in New York, from doctors, nurses, and pharmacists to lawyers, teachers, financial advisors, and commercial drivers, face the same drug possession and sale charges as anyone else (Penal Law Article 220), plus a second case before the agency or board that controls their license. Teen or College Student Arrested for Drugs (CPL Articles 720, 722)

When a teenager is arrested for drugs in New York, Raise the Age (Criminal Procedure Law Article 722) sends 16- and 17-year-olds charged with drug felonies to the Youth Part of Supreme Court, where most nonviolent cases should move to Family Court unless prosecutors show extraordinary circumstances, while misdemeanors and cases involving younger children go to Family Court. College students 18 and older are charged as adults under the same drug laws (Penal Law Article 220), and anyone under 19 may qualify for a sealed youthful offender adjudication (Criminal Procedure Law Article 720). NYCHA Eviction After a Drug Arrest (RPAPL § 715)

A NYCHA eviction after a drug arrest is a termination of tenancy proceeding that the New York City Housing Authority can bring against the leaseholder when police find drugs in an apartment or arrest anyone in the household on drug charges, decided by a NYCHA hearing officer rather than a criminal court judge. Police Searched You Because They Smelled Marijuana (PL § 222.05(3))

Since New York legalized cannabis, police cannot use the smell of marijuana or burnt marijuana, a legal amount of cannabis, or cash found near cannabis as the reason to search you, your bag, or your car (Penal Law § 222.05(3)), with a narrow exception for impaired-driving investigations. Police Illegally Searched You and Found Drugs (CPL § 710.20)

An illegal search happens when police stop, frisk, or search you, your car, or your home without the legal justification the U.S. and New York Constitutions require (N.Y. Const. art. Drugs found in an illegal search can be suppressed at a Mapp or Dunaway hearing (Criminal Procedure Law § 710.20), which often ends the case, because without the drugs prosecutors have nothing left to prove. Prosecutors may allege you consented, the drugs were in plain view, you made a suspicious movement, or an informant’s tip justified the warrant, and the charges that follow can carry up to 364 days in jail on a misdemeanor and three to 20 years in state prison at the highest weights if the search is never challenged. Charged With Selling Drugs for Helping a Friend Buy (Agency Defense)

Being charged with selling drugs for helping a friend buy happens because New York counts almost any hand-off as a sale, but under the agency defense, someone who acted only to help the buyer get drugs, and not on behalf of the seller, is not guilty of selling (People v. Once the defense is properly raised, prosecutors must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that you were working for the seller, which can turn a felony sale charge into misdemeanor possession or less. Prosecutors may allege you were part of the dealer’s operation because you knew where to go, handled the money, kept a little for yourself, or had done it before, often in a buy-and-bust where an undercover officer asked you to get something, and it can carry up to nine years in state prison for a first sale of heroin, cocaine, or fentanyl if the jury never hears the agency defense. Drug Program Instead of Jail: Judicial Diversion and Drug Court (CPL Article 216)

Judicial diversion (Criminal Procedure Law Article 216) lets a judge send someone charged with a class B, C, D, or E drug felony, or certain theft and property felonies, into court-supervised treatment instead of prison, and unlike most deals, it does not require the prosecutor’s consent. People who complete the program can have the charges dismissed or reduced and may qualify for record sealing (Criminal Procedure Law § 160.58), and New York City also runs drug treatment courts and prosecutor-run programs for misdemeanor and felony cases.
