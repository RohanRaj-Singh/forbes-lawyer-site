What it means to prepare a case for trial

Every defense firm says it. Here is what the phrase changes in practice.

Forbes Stranger LLP 6 June 2026 4 min read

“We prepare every case as if it will be tried” is the most common sentence on a criminal defense website. It is also the least examined. Preparation is not an attitude, and it is not a promise to go to trial. It is a set of decisions about how the work is done, and those decisions are visible early.

Discovery is read, not received

In every case, the government produces records, reports, recordings, and laboratory results. The difference between a file and a defense is whether someone has read all of it — not skimmed for the strongest document, but read for the gaps, the inconsistencies, and the things that were never produced at all. That work is unglamorous and it is most of the case.

The theory comes before the evidence

A defense theory is a proposition about what happened and why the government’s account cannot be right. It has to be formed early enough to direct the investigation: which witnesses to find, which records to subpoena, which expert to consult. A theory assembled after discovery closes is usually a comment on the evidence rather than a test of it.

Motions are built from the record

Suppression is not a formality filed at the start of every case. It is a factual claim about how evidence was obtained, and it succeeds or fails on the record made at a hearing. Preparing for trial means treating that hearing as the first one — with the witnesses identified, the sequence established, and the legal question framed before the courtroom fills.

The negotiation changes

A case that can be tried is a case that can be negotiated from a different position. Prosecutors assess risk, and risk is a function of what a defense is actually able to do in front of a jury. The point of readiness is not that trial is always the right answer; it is that the alternative is chosen rather than accepted.

The client’s part

Preparation depends on information only the client has: the full sequence of events, the people involved, the documents that exist, and the consequences that matter most outside the courtroom. It also requires patience — the work is front-loaded, and the visible moments come later. Decisions about plea, testimony, and trial belong to the client; the preparation exists to make those decisions informed.

What it looks like from outside

Very little of it is visible. There is no press release for a case that is ready, and no award for the witness who was found in time. The proof is in what happens when the government’s account is tested — and in the fact that the client was never asked to decide anything without the record in hand.
