Target letters and grand jury subpoenas

In federal matters, the earliest correspondence often decides the shape of the case.

Forbes Stranger LLP 18 August 2026 5 min read

Most federal cases begin quietly. A letter arrives. A subpoena is served on a company. An agent asks whether you would be willing to speak. Nothing has been charged, and it may never be — but the interval between investigation and indictment is where the most consequential decisions in a federal matter are usually made.

What a target letter means

A target letter comes from a federal prosecutor’s office and states that the recipient is considered a target of a grand jury investigation. It often invites the recipient to testify. In federal practice, a target is a person the government believes has committed the crime under investigation; a subject is a person whose conduct is within the scope of the investigation; a witness is neither. These designations are not charges, and they can change. They are, however, a signal about how the government currently sees the matter, and they deserve a considered response rather than a quick one.

What a subpoena requires

A grand jury subpoena is a court order. It requires documents, testimony, or both, and the return date is real. It cannot be ignored, and it should not be answered casually. The scope of a subpoena is often negotiable — the government may accept a narrower production, a different format, or additional time — but that conversation has to happen before the date, through counsel.

Preserve everything, delete nothing

Once an investigation is apparent, records should be preserved, including phones, messaging applications, and personal accounts that may hold responsive material. Destroying or withholding documents is a far more serious problem than producing them, and it converts an investigation into a separate allegation. Preservation is the first instruction in almost every matter of this kind.

The proffer

A proffer is a meeting with prosecutors in which the client answers questions, usually in the hope of persuading the government or earning credit for cooperation. Without an agreement, statements made in a proffer can be used. The terms of any such agreement — what can be used, in what proceeding, and by whom — are negotiated before the meeting, not afterwards.

Testimony before the grand jury

A client may be compelled to appear before the grand jury, and counsel is not permitted inside the room. The decision whether to testify, what to say, and on what basis to decline is one of the few decisions in a federal case that must be made in advance and cannot be revised. It should be made with counsel who has read the subpoena and understands the investigation.

Why the first response matters

The government forms its view of a case early. A response that is organised, accurate, and produced on time reads differently from one that is improvised or late, and the difference persists through charging, negotiation, and sentencing. Engaging counsel before replying is not delay; it is the only opportunity to answer the question the government is actually asking.
